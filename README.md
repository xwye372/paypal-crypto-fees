# buy crypto with PayPal: two real methods compared, fees broken down, and how to avoid the common traps

If you typed "buy crypto with PayPal" into a search box, you're almost certainly trying to figure out one of two things: can I actually do this, and what's it going to cost me. The short answer is yes, you can, and there are two genuinely different ways to go about it — one that's dead simple but restrictive, and one that takes five extra minutes but leaves you with real control over your coins. ## The two ways to buy crypto with PayPal

There's an important distinction most overview articles gloss over. "Buying crypto with PayPal" can mean two completely different things, and the outcome — what you can do with your coins afterward — is not the same.

**Method 1: Buying directly inside the PayPal app.** PayPal lets verified users in certain regions buy, sell, and hold a small selection of cryptocurrencies without ever leaving PayPal's own interface. In the US, that list includes Bitcoin (BTC), Ethereum (ETH), Litecoin (LTC), Bitcoin Cash (BCH), and PayPal's own stablecoin PYUSD. You fund the purchase with your PayPal balance, a linked bank account, or a linked debit card. Credit cards and PayPal Credit are not accepted as funding sources for crypto purchases.

The convenience is real. The drawback is just as real: PayPal does not give you the private keys to what you buy. You hold "rights" to the crypto balance, but you don't hold the crypto in any meaningful self-custody sense. Transferring it out to an external wallet is possible in some regions but subject to limits and network fees, and PayPal's Buyer Protection does not cover cryptocurrency purchases at all.

**Method 2: Using PayPal as a payment method on a crypto exchange.** This is the more flexible route. A growing number of regulated exchanges, including OKX, now let you fund your account or buy crypto directly using your PayPal balance, linked bank account, or card. Because the actual purchase happens on a real exchange, the crypto lands in an account you control. From there you can trade it for hundreds of other assets, move it to a self-custody wallet, or use it in DeFi — none of which the in-app PayPal method easily allows.

For anyone who plans to do anything beyond buying and holding a couple of major coins, Method 2 is the better fit. The rest of this article focuses on how that works in practice, using OKX as the example, while still covering what PayPal charges on its own side.

## What PayPal actually charges when you buy crypto

PayPal's crypto fees are not hidden, but they're not exactly shouted from the rooftops either. On the PayPal side, buying or selling cryptocurrency (other than PYUSD) is subject to a tiered transaction fee based on the USD value of the trade:

| Purchase / sale amount (USD) | PayPal transaction fee |
| --- | --- |
| 1.00 – 74.99 | 2.20% |
| 75.00 – 200.00 | 2.00% |
| 200.01 – 1,000.00 | 1.80% |
| 1,000.01 and above | 1.50% |

On top of that transaction fee, PayPal applies an exchange rate that includes a spread — essentially a markup over the underlying market price. Independent reporting puts that spread roughly in the 1% to 2.4% range depending on the order, though PayPal doesn't publish a single fixed number. So the all-in cost of buying crypto directly inside PayPal is the transaction fee plus whatever is baked into the rate you're quoted.

PYUSD is the exception: there are no transaction fees to buy or sell PayPal's own stablecoin, though a spread still applies and conversion between PYUSD and other supported cryptocurrencies does carry fees.

If you're using PayPal as a funding source on an exchange instead, you typically don't pay PayPal's crypto transaction fee directly — PayPal is functioning as a payment rail, not as the crypto seller. What you pay instead is whatever the exchange and its payment processor charge, which is usually more transparent and often lower overall, especially for larger purchases. More on that in the fees section below.

## Where PayPal fees and exchange fees actually compare

Here's the part that matters for your wallet. When you buy crypto directly inside the PayPal app, you pay PayPal's transaction fee (1.50%–2.20% depending on amount) plus an exchange-rate spread that's typically another 1%–2.4%. That's your all-in cost, and it can land somewhere in the 3%–4.5% range for a typical retail purchase before any bank-side fees.

When you use PayPal to deposit EUR into OKX and then buy on the spot market, the cost structure changes. You pay whatever PayPal charges for the deposit rail (displayed before you confirm), and then you pay OKX's spot trading fee. For a Regular EEA user who has opened the X-Perps account, that's 0.080% maker or 0.100% taker. Even with the deposit processing cost included, the total is usually well below PayPal's in-app crypto fee for anything beyond very small purchases.

The trade-off is that the exchange route requires an account, KYC verification, and a couple of extra clicks. If you're buying $20 of Bitcoin once and never touching it again, PayPal's in-app purchase is genuinely simpler and the fee difference won't ruin your day. If you're buying regularly, trading between coins, or planning to move your crypto to a self-custody wallet, the exchange route wins on both cost and functionality.

## What you can and can't do with crypto bought via PayPal

A few limitations are worth stating plainly because they catch people off guard.

If you buy inside the PayPal app, you do not receive a private key. PayPal grants you rights to the crypto balance, but you can't import it into MetaMask, send it to a hardware wallet on your own terms, or use it in DeFi without going through PayPal's transfer flow — which is region-dependent and subject to network fees. PayPal's Buyer Protection does not apply to crypto purchases, and transactions cannot be cancelled or reversed once confirmed.

In the US, PayPal caps weekly crypto purchases at $100,000 for verified accounts, with a weekly transfer-out limit of $25,000. There's no annual purchase limit. State-level variations apply.

On the OKX side, the main limitation is geographic: PayPal funding is EEA-only and requires full KYC. PayPal is not available as a withdrawal method — you'll use SEPA to pull EUR out. Deposit limits, processing times, and any per-transaction fees are shown on the deposit method selection screen before you commit, so there's no reason to guess.

## Common questions

**Can I buy any cryptocurrency with PayPal?** Only a handful if you buy directly inside PayPal — in the US that's BTC, ETH, LTC, BCH, and PYUSD. On an exchange like OKX, you can use PayPal to fund purchases of a much wider range of assets available on that platform.

**Is it safe to link PayPal to a crypto exchange?** The connection is made through a secure redirect to PayPal's own login page; the exchange doesn't store your PayPal credentials. Enable two-factor authentication on both your PayPal and your exchange accounts for the strongest setup.

**Can I sell crypto and withdraw the cash to PayPal?** On OKX, no — PayPal isn't a supported withdrawal method. You withdraw EUR to your bank via SEPA. On PayPal's own app, you can sell crypto back to PayPal balance.

**What are the fees for buying crypto with PayPal?** Inside PayPal's app: 1.50%–2.20% transaction fee by amount tier, plus an exchange-rate spread. On an exchange: the payment processing cost plus the exchange's trading fee, which is usually lower overall for larger or frequent purchases.

**Can I send crypto I bought on PayPal to an external wallet?** In some regions, yes, subject to limits and network fees. It's not universally available and can be slow. Buying on an exchange and withdrawing from there gives you reliable control.

**Does PayPal Buyer Protection cover crypto?** No. PayPal explicitly states Buyer Protection does not apply to cryptocurrency purchases. You are protected against unauthorized account activity, but not against crypto price moves or purchase errors.
